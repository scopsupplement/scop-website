Most of us think of stress as a feeling. You're stressed or you're not, and a good weekend usually sorts it out. When stress goes on for months, though, it starts to show up in the body as well.

## Cortisol, the stress hormone

Cortisol is the main hormone behind the stress response. It's released by your adrenal glands, and in short bursts it's useful: it gives you energy, sharpens your focus and gets you ready to act. Then it's meant to settle back down.

When stress becomes constant, cortisol stays raised for longer than it should. Over time that can disturb deep sleep, which needs cortisol to be at its lowest. It can affect how your immune system behaves. It's also linked with more fat stored around the middle and with your body handling blood sugar less well.

## Telomeres and ageing

Telomeres are protective caps on the ends of your chromosomes. They shorten a little each time a cell divides, which is why researchers use telomere length as one marker of biological age.

Studies have found that people under long term stress tend to have shorter telomeres than people of the same age who aren't. In one well known group, people caring for a chronically ill family member, the difference was equivalent to roughly a decade of extra ageing.

## Inflammation

Many of the long term effects of stress seem to run through low level inflammation across the body. Researchers measure it with markers such as IL-6 and CRP, and raised levels are linked with heart disease, type 2 diabetes and cognitive decline. Stress can drive inflammation, and inflammation can make stress harder to shake off.

## What helps most

Nutrition can't fix chronic stress, and we won't pretend otherwise. The things with the strongest evidence are sleep, regular exercise, time with people you care about and, where it's needed, proper psychological support. If stress is getting on top of you, talking to your GP is a good first step.
