## Your body's own antioxidant

Most people know antioxidants from food: berries, green tea, vitamin C. But one of the most important antioxidants in your body isn't something you eat. It's something your cells make themselves.

Glutathione is a small molecule built from three amino acids: cysteine, glycine and glutamate. It's described as the most abundant low molecular weight thiol made in our cells, and its job is protection<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. It neutralises reactive oxygen species, helps clear out potentially harmful compounds and keeps the balance between oxidation and reduction inside the cell in check<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>.

## Why training puts it to work

Exercise is good for you, but it also produces more reactive oxygen species in working muscle. A major review of three decades of research found that low levels act as useful signals, helping muscle adapt and switching on the body's own antioxidant defences. Higher levels, particularly during intense or prolonged exercise, are linked to muscle fatigue and reduced force<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>.

That's the balance glutathione helps manage. The aim isn't to wipe out every free radical, which would blunt some of the signals training relies on. It's to keep the system in balance so you recover and go again.

## Where riboflavin comes in

Every time glutathione neutralises an oxidant, it's used up and converted to an oxidised form. To keep working, it has to be recycled back into its active state. The enzyme that does this, glutathione reductase, depends on FAD, a coenzyme made from riboflavin (vitamin B2)<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>.

The link is so direct that the standard blood test for riboflavin status measures the activity of this very enzyme. It's called the erythrocyte glutathione reductase activity coefficient, and a result above 1.4 indicates riboflavin deficiency<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>.

In other words, glutathione does the protecting and riboflavin helps keep it in service. **Riboflavin contributes to the protection of cells from oxidative stress**<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>.

Riboflavin works well beyond that partnership too. **Riboflavin contributes to normal energy-yielding metabolism** and **to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue**<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>.

## Does taking glutathione as a supplement work?

This is where the research is still developing, and it's worth being honest about it.

For a long time it was assumed that glutathione taken by mouth would simply be broken down in the gut. A six month randomised controlled trial in 54 adults challenged that. Taking 250mg or 1,000mg a day increased glutathione stores in the blood, with the higher dose raising levels in red blood cells, plasma and lymphocytes by around 30 to 35%. Levels returned to baseline within a month of stopping<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>.

A shorter trial found something different. Healthy adults taking 1,000mg a day for four weeks showed no significant change in glutathione status or markers of oxidative stress<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup>.

Taken together, the evidence suggests that time matters and that any effect builds gradually rather than overnight. It's an active area of research, and there's no authorised health claim for glutathione in Great Britain, so we won't make one.

## Getting enough riboflavin

The NHS says adults aged 19 to 64 need about 1.3mg of riboflavin a day for men and 1.1mg for women. Good sources include milk, eggs, fortified breakfast cereals, mushrooms and plain yoghurt. Riboflavin is destroyed by UV light, so these foods are best kept out of direct sunlight<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>.

The NHS also notes that taking 40mg or less a day of riboflavin from supplements is unlikely to cause any harm<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>. The body stores only small amounts, and any excess that's absorbed is passed out in urine<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>, which is why it helps to have a steady daily intake.
