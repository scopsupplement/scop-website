## The slow leak

Nobody wakes up at 36 and finds their muscle has gone. That's the problem. It goes quietly, a little at a time, while you're busy with everything else.

Muscle mass decreases by roughly 3 to 8% per decade after the age of 30, and the rate picks up after 60<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. A few percent a decade doesn't sound dramatic. Compounded over thirty years, it's the difference between carrying the shopping in one trip and making two.

The good news is that muscle responds to training at any age. You can't stop the clock, but you can argue with it.

## Why muscle matters more than it looks

Muscle isn't just for the gym mirror. It's what gets you up the stairs, off the floor and through a weekend of DIY without needing a lie down afterwards.

The research on strength training and long term health is hard to ignore. A 2022 meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine pooled 16 cohort studies and found that 30 to 60 minutes a week of muscle strengthening activity was associated with a 10 to 20% lower risk of death from all causes, cardiovascular disease and cancer<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>. People who combined strength work with aerobic exercise saw an even bigger difference, with around a 40% lower risk of death from all causes. These are observational studies, so they show an association rather than proof, but the direction is clear.

What stands out is the dose. The biggest differences appeared at 30 to 60 minutes a week. That's not a new hobby. That's two lunch breaks.

## What the UK guidelines say

The UK Chief Medical Officers recommend that adults build strength on at least two days a week, alongside at least 150 minutes of moderate activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>. Strength gets its own line in the guidelines. It isn't an optional extra.

Most of us focus on the cardio half. Strength is the bit that gets skipped, usually because the weights area looks intimidating or there's a vague belief that lifting is for 22 year olds called Josh.

## How much you actually need

You don't need a complicated programme. You need a few things done consistently:

- **Two or three sessions a week.** Full body sessions work well when time is tight.
- **Big movements.** A squat or leg press, a hinge such as a deadlift or hip thrust, a push, a pull and something for your core. Bodyweight versions count.
- **Progressive overload.** Ask a little more of your muscles over time: an extra rep, a bit more weight or one more set. This is what drives the adaptation.
- **Work close to hard.** Finish most sets with a couple of good reps left in the tank, rather than coasting or grinding out ugly ones.

If you haven't trained for a while, start lighter than your ego would like. Your joints will thank you.

## Recovery is where the work pays off

Training is the stimulus. Recovery is where your body actually adapts. Skimp on it and you'll feel flat, sore and less inclined to come back on Thursday.

**Sleep.** Much of the repair work happens overnight. Cutting sleep to fit in a 5am session is borrowing from the same account you're trying to pay into.

**Protein.** Muscle is built from it. A large meta-analysis found that gains in muscle mass from resistance training levelled off at around 1.6g of protein per kilogram of bodyweight per day<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup>. For most people that means a decent portion of protein at each meal rather than a shaker after every set.

**Rest days.** Give a muscle group a day or two before you train it hard again. Walk, stretch or do some easy [Zone 2 work](zone2-training-longevity.html) in between.

**Micronutrients.** Magnesium **contributes to normal muscle function** and **to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue**, and vitamin D **contributes to the maintenance of normal muscle function**<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>. Neither replaces training, sleep or food, but both are worth having covered, particularly vitamin D through a UK winter.

## Where creatine comes in

Creatine is one of the most researched supplements in sport. Creatine **increases physical performance in successive bursts of short-term, high intensity exercise**, and the beneficial effect is obtained with a daily intake of 3g<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>. A set of squats is a textbook short, high intensity burst.

There's also a claim aimed specifically at older lifters. Daily creatine consumption **can enhance the effect of resistance training on muscle strength in adults over the age of 55**<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>. The beneficial effect is obtained with a daily intake of 3g of creatine alongside regular resistance training that increases the workload over time. The important words are daily and training. Creatine helps the work count. It doesn't do the work for you.
