## The cupboard problem

Most people who take supplements start with good intentions. Creatine for training. Magnesium because someone at the gym swears by it. Vitamin D because it's October again. A B complex for good measure.

Then real life happens. One tub runs out on a Thursday. Another gets left at the back of the cupboard. The 6am alarm wins and the routine quietly slips. A few weeks later you're taking two of the five, on a good day.

It's an easy pattern to fall into, and it matters more than most people realise. For some of the most popular ingredients, what counts isn't any single dose. It's what builds up over weeks of taking it consistently.

## Creatine works by building up

Creatine is a good example. It doesn't work like a pre training drink that kicks in an hour later. Your muscles hold a store of creatine, and daily supplementation gradually tops that store up.

A classic study in men found that taking 3g of creatine a day raised muscle creatine by around 20% over 28 days. That's the same level reached by a short, high dose "loading" phase, just more gradually<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. The same research found that once supplementation stopped, muscle creatine drifted back to where it started within about 30 days<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>.

In other words, the benefit is built by turning up day after day, and it fades when you stop. Creatine **increases physical performance in successive bursts of short-term, high intensity exercise**, and the beneficial effect is obtained with a daily intake of 3g<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>. The key word there is daily. Scōp contains 5,000mg of creatine monohydrate in every scoop.

## Vitamin D reflects weeks, not days

Vitamin D works on a similar timescale. The main marker doctors use to check your vitamin D status, 25(OH)D, has a circulating half-life of around 15 days<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>. Your levels today reflect what you've been doing for the past few weeks, not what you took this morning.

That matters in the UK, where health advice encourages people to consider a vitamin D supplement between October and March<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup>. A tub that gets taken three days a week isn't doing the job it was bought for. Vitamin D **contributes to the normal function of the immune system** and **to the maintenance of normal bones**<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>. ## One missed day won't undo you

Here's the reassuring part. Research on how habits form found that it took people a median of 66 days for a new daily behaviour to become automatic, with a huge range from 18 to 254 days. Crucially, missing a single day did not materially affect the habit forming<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>.

So the goal isn't perfection. A missed scoop on a hectic Monday is fine. What breaks a routine is the stuff that makes it hard to restart: too many products, too many decisions and too many things to reorder.

## Where routines actually break

If you think about why supplement routines fail, it's rarely a lack of motivation. It's friction:

- **Running out.** Five tubs means five different reorder dates. One always runs out first.
- **Too many steps.** Capsules, powders and drops all at different times of day.
- **Decision fatigue.** Which ones today? Did I already take that one?
- **Cost creep.** Separate products add up, and the pricier ones are the first to be dropped.

Every bit of friction you remove makes the next day easier.

## How to make it automatic

A few simple things help a routine stick:

- **Anchor it.** Tie it to something you already do every morning, like boiling the kettle or filling your water bottle.
- **Same time, same place.** Keep it where you'll see it, not at the back of a cupboard.
- **Keep it simple.** The fewer steps, the faster it becomes automatic.
- **Never run out.** Make the reorder somebody else's job.
