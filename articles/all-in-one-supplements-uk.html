All in One Supplements in the UK: Six Things to Check Before You Buy

How to compare all in one supplements in the UK properly: printed doses, UK guidance levels, creatine, ingredient forms, real cost per day and whether you'll actually take it.

If you're shopping for an all in one supplement in the UK, the labels can make every option look much the same. Six checks will tell them apart, and none of them needs a science degree.

What counts as an all in one supplement?

It's a single daily product, usually a powder you mix with water, that replaces a shelf of separate tubs. Most fall into one of two camps.

Greens style powders are wellness led and tend to have long ingredient lists. Targeted daily formulas have fewer ingredients, each chosen for a clear job and dosed to do it. Both camps have good and bad examples, so the type alone won't tell you much.

1. Is every dose printed?

Each ingredient should show its own amount. Some products use a "proprietary blend", which declares the total weight of the blend and leaves you guessing how much of each ingredient is inside. If the dose isn't printed, you can't judge it.

2. Are the key doses meaningful?

A dose needs to be big enough to be worth taking and still sit within UK guidance. These are useful NHS benchmarks for adults:[1][2][3]

Nutrient NHS adult guidance
Vitamin D Up to 100 µg (4,000 IU) a day
Magnesium Up to 400mg a day from supplements
Vitamin B6 Up to 10mg a day from supplements, unless a doctor advises otherwise

Vitamin D deserves a close look in the UK, where the darker months run from autumn through winter.

3. Does it cover performance as well as wellness?

Most all in one powders are built around wellness, so people who train end up buying creatine separately. Creatine is usually taken at around 3g a day. That's far bigger than the microgram amounts of most vitamins, which is why it rarely fits into a capsule or a greens powder.

4. Are the ingredient forms named?

A careful formula tells you which form each nutrient comes in:

  • magnesium as bisglycinate, rather than plain "magnesium"
  • vitamin K2 as MK-7
  • folate as L-methylfolate and vitamin B12 as methylcobalamin
  • vitamin B6 as pyridoxal 5′-phosphate

If the forms aren't named, it's fair to ask the brand why.

5. What does it really cost per day?

Divide the price by the number of servings, then compare subscription price with subscription price. In October 2026, several leading blends worked out at roughly £2.30 to £2.63 a day on subscription.

Check whether you can pause or cancel, and whether a low starter price rises after the first month.

6. Will you actually take it?

Taste matters, as does how easily it mixes and whether it fits your morning. The results come from taking it consistently for weeks, and a supplement you dread drinking won't manage that. There's more on this in The Missed Scoop.
